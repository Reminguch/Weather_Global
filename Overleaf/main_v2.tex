\begin{abstract}
Many existing weather forecasting models are Markovian in their chosen state representation, yet unresolved atmospheric processes can make coarse-grained dynamics history dependent. We introduce GraphCast--Mamba, which augments a frozen, pretrained \(1^\circ\) GraphCast model with a correction branch combining graph message passing and temporal memory. On a development set of 32 matched forecast initializations, selected configurations reduce the GraphCast normalized weighted mean-squared error (MSE; GraphCast loss) by approximately 16\% over a ten-day rollout and 23\% at day 10 (240\,h after initialization). These results highlight the potential of learned non-Markovian corrections for improving weather forecasts at longer lead times.

\section{Introduction}
% Turbulent flows are known as a notoriously difficult framework for numerical simulations. One of the common method in such simulations is the \textit{direct numerical simulations} (DNS) that discretize the Navier-Stokes equation to a space and time grid and then simulate directly. Such discretizations, however require a very fine grid resolution scaling as $N_{\textrm{grid}} \sim \left(Re\right)^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha \sim 2.05 $ \cite{yang2021grid}. Insufficient small-scale resolution may leave mean dissipation and enstrophy approximately converged while substantially underestimating high-order moments of velocity gradients, dissipation, and enstrophy, as well as the tails of velocity-gradient distributions and statistics involving second velocity derivatives \cite{DonzisResolutionEffects}.

Another approach consists in keeping coarse-grained scales, without non-controlled dissipative approximation, but at a cost of keeping the non-Markovian memory terms \cite{YenTingLinMZ2021,de2026data}.

Many numerical and learned forecasters advance a resolved weather state from its current value or a short window of recent values. Examples include the numerical Integrated Forecasting System (IFS) configuration used to produce ERA5~\citep{ecmwf2016ifsdynamics,hersbach2020era5}, and learned Pangu-Weather~\citep{bi2023pangu}, GraphCast~\citep{lam2023graphcast}, FuXi~\citep{chen2023fuxi}, ECMWF's ERA5-native-grid AIFS~\citep{lang2024aifs}, and WeatherNext 2~\citep{alet2025weathernext2}, and WeatherNext 3~\citep{rasp2026weathernext3}. This is a practical representation for autoregressive prediction, but this comes with the limitation that the resolved variables are naturally cut-off at some finite resolution scale. State-of-the-art architectures typically operate at spatial resolutions of approximately \(1^\circ\) (\(\sim 111\) km), \(0.25^\circ\) (\(\sim 28\) km), and \(0.1^\circ\) (\(\sim 11\) km) in latitude/longitude, with the latter used by the recent WeatherNext 3 model. Higher resolution typically improves forecast accuracy, but comes at a dramatically increased computational cost and poses an additional challenge for obtaining sufficiently high-resolution training targets, as observational data are sparse and heterogeneous and global reanalysis products are generally available only at substantially coarser resolutions.

On the other hand, the difficulty of resolving all relevant scales is familiar from turbulent-flow simulation. Direct numerical simulation (DNS) discretizes the governing equations finely enough to represent dynamically active scales, and its grid requirements grow rapidly with Reynolds number. For example, a grid-point estimate for a spatially developing turbulent boundary layer gives $N_{\mathrm{grid}}\sim Re_{L_x}^{2.05}$ under locally adapted DNS spacing~\citep{yang2021grid}. Resolution also affects what a simulation can represent: insufficient small-scale resolution can leave mean dissipation and enstrophy nearly converged while strongly underestimating higher-order gradient statistics and distribution tails~\citep{DonzisResolutionEffects}. Coarser practical models must represent the effects of unresolved motions through an effective closure.

Such a closure need not depend only on the present resolved state. Projecting full dynamics onto a smaller set of variables can yield an instantaneous contribution, a contribution depending on the history of those variables, and a term associated with unobserved initial conditions. The Mori--Zwanzig formalism makes this decomposition explicit; data-driven studies have learned its memory contribution in reduced dynamical systems~\citep{YenTingLinMZ2021,de2026data}. This motivates temporal memory as a possible way to represent effects left out by a finite-window predictor. It does not imply that a learned latent state can be identified with a unique physical memory kernel.

Recent partial differential equation (PDE) experiments provide a concrete example of when memory can help. Buitrago Ruiz and colleagues introduce Memory Neural Operator (MemNO), which inserts a structured state-space sequence (S4) layer within a neural operator and report substantially lower forecast error on coarse or noisy observations. For the Kuramoto--Sivashinsky equation, the advantage of memory over a memoryless operator changes with grid resolution, and the crossover depends on viscosity and the associated high-frequency content~\citep{buitrago2025memory}. Whether an analogous crossover exists for global weather forecasting, and at what grid resolution it might occur, remains an empirical question.

Here, we take a step toward addressing this question by testing whether temporal memory can improve forecasts of some of the frontier models. To test this, we augment a frozen GraphCast model with a trainable Mamba correction branch that combines spatial message passing with Mamba layers. This branch carries a temporal memory state across autoregressive forecast steps and adds a correction to each GraphCast prediction. Learning a residual correction focuses training on what the predictor misses instead of requiring a new model to relearn its dominant dynamics.

In particular, we use the following ansatz for the predictor:
\begin{equation}
    (c_t,m_t)=R_\phi(X_t,m_{t-1}),
    \qquad
    \widehat{Y}_t=G_\theta(X_t)+c_t,
    \qquad \theta\ \text{fixed},
    \label{eq:intro_memory_correction}
\end{equation}
where $G_\theta$ is the pretrained GraphCast backbone, $R_\phi$ is the trainable Mamba correction branch, and $c_t$ is its forecast correction. The temporal memory state $m_t$ persists between forecast calls and comprises all state-space model (SSM) states and causal-convolution caches in the branch. 

Output corrections to frozen time-series forecasters have been studied in $\delta$-Adapter~\citep{liang2026forecast}, and neural corrections have been applied during the integration of a numerical weather model~\citep{chen2022correcting}. Memory within a forecaster is also established, including recurrent global weather models~\citep{hu2023swinvrnn} and the neural-operator approach above. These precedents motivate a more specific question: can a separate correction branch improve an already trained state-of-the-art forecaster by carrying temporal memory across its autoregressive forecast steps?

The predictor and correction meet at the output, so this formulation does not require access to the predictor's internal layers. In our weather instantiation, $G_\theta$ is a frozen GraphCast model~\citep{lam2023graphcast} and $R_\phi$ combines graph message passing with Mamba layers~\citep{gu2023mamba}. A zero-initialized output head makes the GraphCast--Mamba model start from the GraphCast forecast; only the correction branch is then trained.

We test this instantiation at $1^\circ$ resolution on 32 matched forecast initializations from a development set. The selected correction reduces the exact GraphCast loss by $16.34\%$ over ten-day rollouts and $23.68\%$ at day 10. Separate memory-reset controls, measured with an approximate reconstruction of that loss, support a contribution from persistent temporal context beyond the branch's additional trainable capacity. These results establish a case for memory-based correction of a strong pretrained forecaster at the tested resolution. The resolution at which its memory benefit might diminish, and whether the same correction strategy transfers to other forecasters, require separate matched experiments.

\section{A hybrid GraphCast--Mamba architecture}

% suozhi 采纳的建议：Method 优先解释本文的训练流程，通用 Mamba 推导可缩短或移到附录。

We study whether temporal memory improves forecasts beyond the finite input history supplied to a pretrained GraphCast model. GraphCast uses two successive atmospheric states as input; its forecast is Markovian with respect to this augmented state. Figure~\ref{fig:GC_Mamba_rollout} shows how a separate trainable branch corrects each GraphCast forecast and carries memory across successive six-hour steps. The GraphCast--Mamba prediction advances the meteorological input window, while the branch's temporal memory retains information from the preceding trajectory. Figure~\ref{fig:GC_Mamba_correction} details how graph message passing mixes information across mesh nodes and Mamba updates memory independently at each node. This introduces history dependence relative to the baseline input representation.

\paragraph{GraphCast}
The baseline is a pretrained GraphCast checkpoint. This graph neural network operates on a latitude--longitude grid and an auxiliary mesh, with graph $\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{V}^{\textrm{G}},\mathcal{V}^{\textrm{M}},\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{G2M}},\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{M}},\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{M2G}})$. The vertices $\mathcal{V}^{\textrm{G}}$ represent the meteorological grid and $\mathcal{V}^{\textrm{M}}$ the mesh. Encoding uses grid-to-mesh edges $\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{G2M}}$, followed by $N=16$ graph message-passing steps over $\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{M}}$ and decoding over mesh-to-grid edges $\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{M2G}}$. Latent node features have width $W_{\textrm{GC}}=512$. The mesh provides a more compact representation for spatial processing than the meteorological grid.

\paragraph{Mamba}
Mamba is an attention-free sequence model based on selective state-space models (SSMs), designed to model long temporal dependencies with computation that scales linearly with sequence length \citep{gu2023mamba}. A conventional continuous-time SSM maps an input sequence $x(t)$ to an SSM state $h(t)$ according to
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d h(t)}{dt} = A h(t) + B x(t),
    \qquad
    y(t) = C h(t) + D x(t).
\end{equation}
Here, $A$ is a diagonal matrix that determines how quickly memory decays or evolves, $B$ and $C$ determines how new inputs are written into the state and readout from the state respectively, and $D$ provides a direct input-to-output skip connection. For a single SSM channel, each of the matrices have fixed dimension $d_{\textrm{state}}=16$ and $\Delta$ - is a scalar. If $A$, $B$, and $C$ are fixed, the same linear dynamical system is applied at every time step. For a discrete step size $\Delta$, its exact discretization is
\begin{equation}
    h_t = \bar{A} h_{t-1} + \bar{B}x_t,
    \qquad
    \bar{A} = \exp(\Delta A),
    \qquad
    \bar{B} = A^{-1}\left[\exp(\Delta A)-I\right]B.
\end{equation}

Mamba makes this SSM \emph{selective}: rather than using fixed input and readout matrices and a fixed step size, it computes $B_t$, $C_t$, and $\Delta_t$ from the current input. Thus, the model can decide which information to retain, overwrite, or expose at each time step.

In our model, Mamba operates independently on the temporal sequence of each GraphCast mesh node. Let $x_t^{\mathrm{in}}\in\mathbb{R}^{W_{\textrm{GC}}}$ denote the mesh latent feature at a given node and forecast step. A layer-normalized input is projected into two $d_{\textrm{inner}}$-dimensional branches,
\begin{equation}
    [\tilde{x}_t,z_t]
    =
    W_{\mathrm{in}}\operatorname{LN}(x_t^{\mathrm{in}}),
    \qquad
    \tilde{x}_t,z_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\textrm{inner}}}.
\end{equation}
The first branch, $\tilde{x}_t$, supplies the SSM input. The second branch, $z_t$, is a learned output gate: it does not update the SSM state directly, but controls how much of the SSM output is passed onward.

Before entering the SSM, $\tilde{x}_t$ is processed by a depthwise causal one-dimensional convolution with kernel width four:
\begin{equation}
    u_{t,d}
    =
    \operatorname{SiLU}
    \left(
        b^{\mathrm{conv}}_d
        +
        \sum_{k=0}^{d_{\textrm{conv}}-1}
        w^{\mathrm{conv}}_{d,k}\tilde{x}_{t-k,d}
    \right),
    \qquad d=1,\ldots, d_{\textrm{inner}},
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{SiLU}(a)=a/(1+e^{-a})$. ``Depthwise'' means that each of the $d_{inner}$ channels has its own $d_{\textrm{conv}}$ learned convolution weights $w^{\mathrm{conv}}_{d,k}$ and does not mix with other channels at this stage. ``Causal'' means that $u_t$ depends only on the current input and the three previous inputs, never on future inputs. During autoregressive inference, the preceding three projected inputs are retained in a convolution cache, so this local temporal context continues across forecast calls.

The selective parameters are generated from $u_t$ by learned linear projections:
\begin{equation}
    [r_t,B_t,C_t] = W_xu_t,
    \qquad
    r_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d_t},
    \quad
    B_t,C_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\textrm{state}}},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_t
    =
    \operatorname{softplus}(W_\Delta r_t+b_\Delta)
    \in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\textrm{inner}}}.
\end{equation}
Here $d_t=32$ and $d_{\mathrm{state}}=16$. The projection matrices, convolution kernels, and biases are trainable parameters optimized using the forecasting loss. In contrast, $B_t$, $C_t$, and $\Delta_t$ are input-dependent values generated anew at each time step from $u_t$. The softplus function ensures $\Delta_t>0$.

The continuous-time state matrix is diagonal and parameterized as
\begin{equation}
    A_{d,n}=-\exp(A_{\log,d,n}),
    \qquad
    d=1,\ldots,d_{\textrm{inner}},\quad n=1,\ldots,d_{\textrm{state}}.
\end{equation}
Because every element of $A$ is negative, the corresponding factor $\exp(\Delta_{t,d}A_{d,n})$ lies between zero and one, giving stable, decaying memory. The implementation uses the following selective recurrence:
\begin{equation}
    h_{t,d,n}
    =
    \exp\!\left(\Delta_{t,d}A_{d,n}\right)h_{t-1,d,n}
    +
    \Delta_{t,d}B_{t,n}u_{t,d}.
\end{equation}
The first term retains a scaled portion of the previous state, while the second writes the current input into memory. A small $\Delta_{t,d}$ gives slow decay and long memory; a large $\Delta_{t,d}$ gives faster decay and a stronger update from the current input.

The state is read out using the selective vector $C_t$ and a learned skip parameter $D$:
\begin{equation}
    s_{t,d}
    =
    \sum_{n=1}^{16} C_{t,n}h_{t,d,n}
    +
    D_d u_{t,d}.
\end{equation}
Finally, the gate branch $z_t$ modulates this readout, and the result is projected back to the $W_{\textrm{GC}}$-dimensional GraphCast latent space:
\begin{equation}
    o_t
    =
    W_{\mathrm{out}}
    \left[
        s_t\odot\operatorname{SiLU}(z_t)
    \right],
    \qquad
    x_t^{\mathrm{out}}=x_t^{\mathrm{in}}+o_t.
\end{equation}

The principal experiments use $d_{\mathrm{inner}}=16$, an SSM state dimension of $16$ per channel, a causal convolution width of $4$, and a rank-$32$ projection for $\Delta_t$; we also examine $d_{\mathrm{inner}}=32$ and $64$. The SSM state $h_t$ and the three-token convolution cache persist separately for each batch element, mesh node, and Mamba layer across autoregressive forecast steps. The equations above describe one shared group of input-dependent $B_t,C_t$ coefficients. The grouped variant partitions the inner channels into groups with separate $B_t,C_t$ coefficients; the temporal-memory ablation compares one and four such groups.



\paragraph{An additive correction with temporal memory.}
Let $X_t$ denote the two-frame meteorological input and the associated forcings, and let $m_t$ collect the SSM states and convolution caches of all Mamba layers. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:GC_Mamba_rollout}, the forecast combines the frozen GraphCast backbone $G_\theta$ with a trainable correction $R_\phi$:
\begin{equation}
    (c_t,m_t)=R_\phi(X_t,m_{t-1}),
    \qquad
    \hat{Y}_t=G_\theta(X_t)+c_t,
    \qquad \theta\ \text{fixed}.
    \label{eq:additive_correction}
\end{equation}
The correction branch in Figure~\ref{fig:GC_Mamba_correction} has its own grid-to-mesh encoder, mesh processor, and mesh-to-grid decoder. It receives the same meteorological inputs as the baseline and uses separate parameters throughout. Its mesh processor uses two graph message-passing steps, each followed by a two-layer Mamba module, giving four recurrent layers in total. Encoder grid features are also passed directly to the decoder. Suppressing this grid-feature connection in the notation, the mesh-processing path is
\begin{align}
    L_t^{(0)} &= \operatorname{Encode}_\phi(X_t),\\
    \widetilde{L}_t^{(k)} &= \operatorname{GNN}^{(k)}_\phi(L_t^{(k-1)}),\\
    (L_t^{(k)},m_t^{(k)})
    &=\operatorname{MambaModule}^{(k)}_\phi
       (\widetilde{L}_t^{(k)},m_{t-1}^{(k)}),\qquad k=1,2,\\
    c_t &= \operatorname{Decode}_\phi(L_t^{(2)}).
\end{align}
Each Mamba module contains the residual Mamba updates described above; $m_t^{(k)}$ comprises the SSM states and convolution caches of its two layers, while $h_t$ denotes an individual SSM state. The output head is initialized to zero, so the initial GraphCast--Mamba forecast equals the GraphCast forecast. During autoregressive forecasting, the oldest input frame is dropped, the GraphCast--Mamba prediction $\hat{Y}_t$ is appended, and the known forcings are updated to form $X_{t+1}$. The temporal memory state is passed separately to the next correction call. The cold-start rollout illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:GC_Mamba_rollout} begins with $m_{-1}=0$ and retains the updated temporal memory state thereafter.

\subsection{Improved skill at longer forecast lead times}
Figure~\ref{fig:res1_forecast} shows the $d_{\mathrm{inner}}=16$ correction trained with a $3\times$ Mamba learning rate and SWA. It reduces exact aggregate ten-day loss by $16.34\%$ and day-10 loss by $23.68\%$. The per-lead reduction grows from $3.45\%$ at day 4 to $16.25\%$ at day 7 and $23.68\%$ at day 10. Day-1 loss reduction is $-0.13\%$, indicating a small degradation; short-lead skill is largely preserved rather than uniformly improved. At day 10, $2$\,m temperature RMSE is $2.29$\,K, a $10.98\%$ reduction relative to the paired baseline.

\subsection{Evidence for useful temporal memory}
\label{sec:memory_ablation}
% suozhi 采纳的建议：突出记忆对照，用表格区分训练与预测时的状态策略，并明确 SSM 与卷积缓存的归因边界。
\suozhi{Please give the memory controls a more prominent role in the argument. A small table should show whether carry/carry, carry/reset, and reset/reset retain memory during training and forecasting, and which control is trained separately. Explain how the controls distinguish additional model capacity from persistent memory. Since resetting clears both the SSM state and convolution cache, the current study does not isolate the contribution of long-term SSM memory.}

A correction branch can improve forecasts through additional spatial capacity as well as temporal memory. To distinguish these contributions, we compare three policies: state carry during both training and forecasting; state carry during training followed by resetting at every forecast step; and a separately trained model with resetting at every training and forecast step. Resetting zeros both the SSM state and the convolution cache. The carry-trained and reset-trained runs have the same architecture, data settings, optimizer, and training seed; their training/forecasting memory policy differs.

Figure~\ref{fig:memory_reset} isolates the training/forecasting memory policy for a fixed $d_{\mathrm{inner}}=16$ model. Carry/carry gives the strongest improvement at longer forecast lead times: day-10 approximate loss reduction is $22.52\%$, compared with $9.24\%$ for carry/reset and $12.91\%$ for reset/reset. Retaining memory therefore adds $13.28$ percentage points relative to resetting the trained state during forecasting and $9.62$ percentage points relative to training and forecasting without persistent temporal memory. Additional group settings and averaging windows are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:memory_windows}.

The forecast-time reset demonstrates dependence on the learned state, but also introduces a training--forecasting mismatch. The separately trained reset control is therefore essential: it retains the trainable correction architecture while removing persistent temporal context. Its smaller benefit at longer forecast lead times supports a contribution from memory beyond additional correction capacity. This is evidence for useful history dependence relative to the baseline's two-frame input representation. The experiment does not identify a unique physical memory kernel or isolate the SSM contribution from that of the convolution cache.

\paragraph{Scope of the evidence.}
The exact-loss studies use one training seed and a 32-initialization development set used in model selection. We therefore report descriptive improvements without significance claims. The memory controls support the contribution of persistent state in the tested configuration; they do not directly establish the same attribution for every model. Evaluation on forecast dates reserved from selection, with matched uncertainty estimates and exact-loss reset controls, would strengthen the quantitative conclusions.

\section{Training choices and checkpoint averaging}
\label{app:training}
\paragraph{Training methods and model selection.}
Table~\ref{tab:res1_skill} reports four SWA configurations from the Mamba learning-rate study: equal spatial and Mamba learning rates and a $3\times$ Mamba learning rate, each with inner dimensions 16 and 32. The equal-rate configuration applies the same learning rate to both parameter groups; the $3\times$ configuration increases only the Mamba rate. The pretrained GraphCast parameters remain frozen in all cases. Figure~\ref{fig:res1_forecast} uses the dimension-16, $3\times$ Mamba learning-rate SWA configuration, which has the largest aggregate and day-10 exact-loss reductions among the completed SWA configurations in the reported optimizer and learning-rate studies. This selection uses the matched 32-initialization development evaluation and does not establish optimality on an independent test set.

\paragraph{Optimizer study.}
The optimizer study varies peak learning rate ($10^{-4}$ or $5\times10^{-5}$), Adam moments, temporal initialization scheme (legacy or Mamba1), and the paired inner-dimension/group settings (16/1 or 64/4). The latter changes two factors simultaneously and does not isolate inner dimension. Runs use 10,000 updates, seed 22, a 200-update warm-up, cosine decay to one tenth of the peak learning rate, and weight decay $10^{-4}$. The correction is trained with closed-loop feedback, 24-step backpropagation windows, a 20-step autoregressive tail, and loss across all 24 steps. The correction contribution to the feedback is stop-gradient, while temporal memory state gradients propagate within the backpropagation window.

Each run screens periodic checkpoints and three SWA windows on eight forecast initializations. The selected checkpoint and SWA are then evaluated on the matched 32 forecast initializations. Selection uses these development evaluations, so the study does not represent an untouched test-set comparison.

Day-10 reductions for the 22 completed selected SWA configurations range from $13.47\%$ to $23.21\%$. The configuration at the upper end uses Mamba1 initialization, inner dimension 16, one $B,C$ group, learning rate $10^{-4}$, and AdamW moments $(0.9,0.98)$; its aggregate loss reduction is $16.28\%$.

\paragraph{Mamba learning rate and inner dimension.}
The Mamba learning-rate study keeps spatial width 512 and one $B,C$ group while varying inner dimension and the Mamba learning-rate multiplier. Each arm uses its own 500-update warm-up followed by 2,000 post-warm-up updates at batch size four. The base peak learning rate is $10^{-4}$ with AdamW moments $(0.9,0.98)$. Table~\ref{tab:lr_sensitivity} extends the four configurations in Table~\ref{tab:res1_skill} with the $0.3\times$ Mamba learning rate at each inner dimension. All six use SWA over post-warm-up updates 500, 1,000, 1,500, and 2,000. The warm-up parameter values differ across arms despite the common training seed, so the comparisons describe complete training runs rather than isolated learning-rate interventions.

Under equal spatial and Mamba learning rates, the dimension-32 SWA correction exceeds the dimension-16 correction's aggregate loss reduction by $0.62$ percentage points. Under the $3\times$ Mamba learning rate, the dimension-32 correction instead has a smaller aggregate reduction by $0.61$ percentage points. The $0.3\times$ Mamba learning-rate SWA results also favor dimension 32, while each dimension attains its largest reductions at a different multiplier: $3\times$ for dimension 16 and $1\times$ for dimension 32. These observations do not establish a monotonic benefit from increasing inner dimension or Mamba learning rate.

\section{Training tail length and the breadth of forecast gains}
\label{app:training_horizon}
This appendix examines the archived v22 training-tail sweep, whose saved evaluations each contain 32 forecast initializations and 40 six-hour forecast steps. These historical models are distinct from the configurations in the exact-loss studies in the main text. We report the archived weighted-MSE diagnostic for the training-tail comparison and latitude-weighted RMSE for the variable comparison.

\subsection{Dependence on training tail length}
Previous neural-operator studies, including MemNO and Factorized Fourier Neural Operators (F-FNO), have demonstrated effective forecasting using teacher forcing, where predictions are conditioned on ground-truth states during training~\citep{buitrago2025memory,tran2023factorized}. MemNO retains a history of these states: its next-step supervision therefore includes temporal context. Here we examine how forecast skill depends on the training tail label $K$. We retain the historical labeling convention: $K=1$ denotes fully teacher-forced training, whereas $K>1$ denotes $K$ feedback-conditioned steps in each 24-step training window. The latter follow a teacher-forced prefix, so the longest chain includes $K+1$ predictions, beginning with the final prediction from an ERA5 input.

Figure~\ref{fig:training_ar_horizon} reveals a strong dependence on training tail length. Shorter training tails primarily improve early forecasts, with gains diminishing as the forecast advances. Longer training tails produce substantially larger improvements at longer forecast lead times, with a pronounced crossover between $K=16$ and $K=18$ in the present sweep. This behavior suggests that sufficiently long training tails are important for realizing the correction model's benefit at longer forecast lead times. We interpret this as an empirical crossover for this configuration; establishing a universal critical training tail length would require further experiments.

\subsection{Improvements across forecast variables}
For the $K=22$ model, the gains extend across the predicted atmospheric state. Figure~\ref{fig:channels_improved_k22} shows that the number of channels with lower latitude-weighted RMSE than GraphCast increases with forecast lead time, reaching 81 of 83 channels at day 4 and remaining at 82--83 channels from approximately day 5 onward. All 83 channels improve at the evaluated leads from 204 to 234\,h; 82 improve at 240\,h, with 50\,hPa temperature the remaining exception.

At day 10, all 11 reported variable groups have lower RMSE, with reductions ranging from $4.55\%$ to $9.66\%$. The improvements span temperature, geopotential, mean sea-level pressure, specific humidity, precipitation, and wind, including pressure vertical velocity. The distinction between variable groups and individual pressure-level channels matters: improvement in every group can coexist with a small number of unimproved channels. Together, these diagnostics show that the gains at longer forecast lead times extend broadly across the forecast variables.
